# low-ban design

Low bandwidth video experiment. Three panes compare the raw webcam feed, low-ban - a
face-prioritised hybrid codec whose receiver moves the face it holds with transmitted
landmarks - and an H.264 baseline given the same bitrate and the same face boxes. The premise
is that non-verbal communication survives extreme compression if scarce bits are concentrated
around expression-bearing regions and the face's motion travels as landmarks rather than pixels.

The presentation is C++20 on libdesktop's `core`, `platform`, `render` and `ui` libraries: a
`ui::shell` whose native-caption frame holds a row of two toolbar buttons over a canvas element
that paints through `render::draw_context`. Camera capture, clip recording and the H.264
baseline use Windows' Media Foundation - its source reader, sink writer and in-box H.264 encoder
and decoder; there is no ONNX or DirectML runtime and no Windows AI API. Faces come from
[libmug](https://github.com/ZacWalk/libmug), which runs libfacedetection's YuNet on its own SIMD
kernels and gives every face a box and five landmarks; the expression prior that steers the
codec's bits was learned offline (see the [README](../README.md#credits)). This is a demo: the
goal is to be small and legible, not to be robust in every lighting condition.

## Layout

```
src/

  engine.h/.cpp   Threads, capture and clip recording, publishing results.

  capture.h/.cpp  Media Foundation webcam capture, clip recording and replay, y4m replay; stills over WIC.
  image.h/.cpp    Image containers, YUY2 and NV12 conversion, resize, blur, Sobel.
  face.h/.cpp     Face detection through libmug, and the face importance map.
  expression-prior.cpp  The learned expression prior, generated by tools/learn_expression_prior.py.
  landmarks.h/.cpp Landmark jitter filter and landmark error.
  warp.h/.cpp     Integer Delaunay triangulation and piecewise-affine face warp.
  entropy.h/.cpp  Adaptive binary range coder and Exp-Golomb binarisation.
  codec.h/.cpp    The low-ban wire - landmarks and picture in one packet - and quality metrics.
  baseline.h/.cpp H.264 baseline wire over Media Foundation's encoder and decoder.
  evaluate.cpp    Offline evaluation of PNG stills and clips.
  selftest.cpp    Headless checks (`/test`).
samples/          stills, and clips used by `dd.ps1 evaluate` (git-ignored)
tests/            the embedded-manifest check and a trivial CTest executable
tools/            the expression prior's learning tool and the icon's generator
dd.psd1           dd settings, and the `evaluate` command (cmake/project-command.ps1)
```

Dependency direction is strictly one way: `main` depends on `engine`, `engine` on `session`
and `capture`, `session` on the processing modules, and the processing modules on `image`.
`main` depends on libdesktop's `ui` layer, with rendering through `render` and platform
services through `platform`; it does not name an `HWND`, GDI object or private Win32 facade.
`image`, `entropy` and `warp` have no Windows dependency at all, which is what makes the self
test possible without a camera or a window. `baseline` and `capture` hide Media Foundation
behind Windows-free headers.

## Threading

| Thread   | Responsibility |
| -------- | -------------- |
| capture  | Blocks on `IMFSourceReader::ReadSample`, decodes YUY2 into planes, publishes the frame, and writes it to a clip while recording. |
| analysis | Finds faces and their landmarks, runs the call session, publishes a `render_state`. Owns the H.264 transforms, so it initialises COM, and the one face detector, which it also uses to find the faces again in what the receivers show. |
| UI       | The app is a `ui::shell`, which makes the frame, while `platform::run` drives the native window/message loop. Its 16 ms application frame tick checks the generation counter and queues one invalidation; the shared paint pass draws the three panes, captions, status and face boxes through one `render::draw_context`. |

Handover is by `std::shared_ptr` to an immutable value, swapped under a short mutex. A
consumer either sees the previous frame in full or the next frame in full, never a mixture,
and neither producer ever blocks on the consumer. The UI thread only converts the
already-published panes to `core::surface` values and draws them; it does no codec, camera,
face or landmark processing, so a slow analysis pass degrades the frame rate rather than
freezing the window. Nothing measures how long that pass takes, and it has no time budget.

There is no menu. A `ui::toolbar` of two checkable `ui::command` buttons sits above the canvas:
Record clip, and Face priority, which switches face detection off. Each command keeps how to
read its setting, and the frame tick re-reads both, so a recording a camera error stopped shows
as stopped. The shell reopens the window where it was closed, or centred, closes it through the
app lifecycle, and holds it with `ui::shell_options::minimum_client` to a client area that fits
the buttons and all three 640x480 panes and their captions. The shell scales sizes with the
display, as it does the buttons, but the panes are drawn a pixel to a pixel, so their physical
sizes are divided by that scale first.

The analysis thread compares `shared_ptr` identity to skip frames it has already handled,
which is also what keeps it from spinning when the camera stalls.

The `platform::run` UI thread is STA, but `/test`, `/evaluate` and `/probe-h264` run their
Media Foundation and WIC work through the capture backend's fresh native MTA boundary. This
keeps console-mode COM apartment behaviour equal to the GUI path without putting COM
initialisation in the GUI layer.

## Capture

`webcam` requests YUY2 at 640x480. YUY2 is what webcams natively produce, so there is no
conversion cost. The face detector reads its luma where it lies, at video range.

`decode_yuy2` splits the packed `Y0 Cb Y1 Cr` macropixels into three planes: full resolution
luma and half width chroma. A negative `MF_MT_DEFAULT_STRIDE` means a bottom-up buffer and is
normalised to top-down during the split, so nothing downstream has to think about it.

The negotiated subtype is checked after `SetCurrentMediaType`, and opening fails with the
actual FOURCC if the camera substituted something else. Everything downstream unpacks YUY2 by
hand, so a silent substitution would appear as noise rather than as a failure.

A camera that opens and then stops delivering - held by another application, unplugged - fails
`ReadSample` or flags an error or the end of the stream. The capture thread then stops and
reports why on the window's status line, as it does when no camera opens at all, instead of
retrying a reader that is finished. Setting a status moves the generation counter as a frame
does: with no frames arriving, nothing else would make the window repaint, and the status
would stay off screen.

### Recorded clips

The Record clip button writes the camera to `samples/clip-<local time>.mp4` until it is pressed
again. Frames are written on an even 15 Hz cadence and stamped at exact intervals, so a clip
replays at precisely its frame rate whatever the camera's own timing was. The picture is packed
back into YUY2 and handed to a Media Foundation sink writer as H.264 at 4 Mbit/s - about 0.9
bits per pixel, high enough to be a faithful source for both wires (the self test requires
better than 30 dB on a round trip). A clip is finished when recording stops, when the camera
fails and when the app closes, because an unfinished MP4 cannot be played back.

`clip_reader` replays a clip as captured frames through the source reader's advanced video
processor, as YUY2 or, failing that, NV12, at 640x480 whatever size it was recorded at. It also
reads YUV4MPEG2 (`.y4m`) streams with 4:2:0 chroma - the format the standard video test
sequences are published in - straight from the file, a frame at a time: the middle of the
frame at the capture's 4:3 shape, as a camera of that shape would frame it, scaled to 640x480
by area averaging where it shrinks and linearly where it grows, stamped at the stream's own
frame rate.

## The call session

`call_session` is one simulated call. Every captured frame goes in with its timestamp in
seconds and the face analysis; out comes what both receivers show. The live engine passes
steady-clock time and `evaluate` passes clip timestamps, so the offline numbers are measured on
exactly the code the window runs.

The sender's analysis - `analyse_faces` - finds the faces in each frame's luma with libmug,
each with a box and five landmarks: the eye centres, the tip of the nose and the corners of the
mouth (see [face detection](#face-detection)). The landmarks drive everything else: the
importance map, the warp, and the landmark error. The importance map gives background pixels
weight 32 and each detected face's box weight 96, then raises the face towards 255 by the
[expression prior](#the-expression-prior), placed on the face by its five landmarks: what moves
in a face beyond what those five points say has to come through the picture, so that is where
the picture's bits go.

Pictures are coded at half the capture size in each direction, 320x240, and both receivers
enlarge theirs again with the same Catmull-Rom bicubic filter for display and measurement.
Landmarks are found at the full 640x480 and sent rounded to the coded picture's pixels. Every
practical low-rate system drops resolution first: at 20 kbit/s a 15 Hz packet averages 167
bytes, 0.017 bits per pixel of the coded picture.

One clock runs at 15 Hz, on an even cadence with a few milliseconds of slack so a 30 fps camera
lands on every tick rather than slipping to every third frame. Each tick sends at most one
low-ban packet - landmarks and picture together - and one H.264 frame. Both wires are held to
one payload rate, `call_kbit_per_second`, 20 kbit/s: H.264's constant bit rate, and the rate
low-ban's token bucket fills at.

### Rate control

Low-ban's bucket gains the call rate's share every tick, 167 bytes, and holds at most 1,200 -
one packet that never needs fragmenting - and no packet after the startup keyframe is larger. A
packet spends what the bucket holds. Landmarks always go, so a packet that is only new landmarks
can leave the bucket a few bytes in debt. A still scene sends little or nothing and banks the
rest for the next movement.

What a packet is worth spending on is set by its *quality*, the background's quantiser step
(see [quantisers](#quantisers)). The session moves it one step a tick: coarser when macroblocks
worth sending were left for want of bytes and the bucket is all but dry, finer when nothing was
left and the bucket is more than half full. The startup keyframe codes at step 12, a quantiser
of 16, and the quality then ranges between steps 9 and 36.

The window shows three caption lines under each received pane: rate, quality, and what the last
packet was. Rates are rolling ten-second averages of serialized bytes - low-ban's whole packets,
with the landmarks' share shown apart, and the encoder's output for H.264 - and include and then
age out each wire's startup picture.

### The link estimate

Payload is not what a link has to carry. Each pane also shows a link figure: payload plus 40
bytes of IPv4, UDP and RTP header on every packet, plus an allowance for the audio a real call
cannot do without - 6 kbit/s of low-rate speech in 60 ms packets, 11.3 kbit/s with its headers.
Nothing is sent through a socket; this is accounting, not a network measurement, and it excludes
retransmission, error correction and congestion. Both wires send at most 15 packets a second,
4.8 kbit/s of headers; low-ban sends none on a tick with nothing to say.

## Low-ban

Low-ban's wire, `lowban_wire` in `codec.h`, is a block-based hybrid codec in the broad family of
H.263 and H.264, built around three ideas of its own: the face the receiver holds moves with five
transmitted landmarks before any block is coded; macroblocks compete for a packet's bytes on
distortion removed per bit, tilted towards faces; and quantisers follow the importance map. Its
coding tools are H.264's, cut down: 16x16 macroblocks of four 8x8 transform blocks, each
skipped along a predicted vector, moved by quarter-pixel vectors - one for the macroblock or
one for each block - or predicted intra along nine directions, and a loop filter.

### Packet scheduling

Far more of the frame wants to change each tick than the bucket can carry. The wire resolves
that with one rule, and the rule is the single most important thing in this repository.

A 16x16 macroblock competes on

```
priority = importance_gain * distortion_removed / bits
```

`distortion_removed` is how much squared pixel error the macroblock's best record would actually
retire, measured against what the decoder holds rather than against the source. `bits` is that
record's cost estimated against the entropy coder's current models. `importance_gain` runs from
1 to 8 across the importance map. A macroblock is a candidate at all only when its record is
worth its bits: when its distortion plus lambda times its bits beats leaving it alone (see
[rate-distortion decisions](#rate-distortion-decisions)).

The gain being *bounded* is the point. An importance term that can outweigh distortion
outright does not prioritise the face, it starves everything else forever, and the visible
symptom is a head that leaves copies of itself behind: the region the face moved off is now
low importance, so no matter how wrong it is it never outbids the face that keeps changing at
its new position.

A bounded gain is still not enough on its own, because the face regenerates high-priority
candidates every tick. The wire therefore also counts, for each macroblock, how many packets in
a row it has been a candidate left unsent. At sixteen packets, about a second, it is promoted
ahead of every macroblock still inside its deadline, and the promoted set is ordered among
itself by the same rule. Importance decides the order in which macroblocks are served; it can
never decide that a macroblock is not served.

Selection and serialization are separate passes. Macroblocks are chosen by priority on
estimated bits, then coded in raster order. The range coder knows its exact length at every
point, so while the packet is over its budget the least deserving choices are dropped and the
records coded again; what is sent never exceeds the budget.

### Quantisers

Quantisers double every six steps from 4 at step 0, as H.264's do. A macroblock's step is the
packet's quality, the background's step, made finer by up to nine steps - a factor of 2.8 - in
proportion to how far the importance of its most important 8x8 block rises above the
background's: a face box's 96 is three steps finer, the expression prior's peak nine. Taking
the most important block, not the mean, gives a face that only clips a macroblock the face's
quantiser there. Steps are whole, so the importance map drifting by a pixel with the face
rarely changes a quantiser. Higher spatial frequencies are quantised progressively more
coarsely, `1 + 0.12 (u + v)` times the macroblock's quantiser. A macroblock sends its step
only when one of its blocks carries a residual, and each 8x8 block keeps the step its residual
was last coded at, which is what the [loop filter](#the-loop-filter) reads.

### The landmark warp

When a packet carries new landmarks, both ends can move the face they hold to where the
landmarks now put it before any block is coded. Each face's five landmarks, plus a ring of
twelve points placed from its eyes and mouth alone - the hairline, the temples, the cheeks, the
jaw and the chin, as `face_mesh_points` reaches them on libmug's template face - are
triangulated where they are now, and every pixel inside that mesh is sampled from the matching
triangle where they were when the held picture's face was drawn. The ring moves the whole face
with the head; the five landmarks inside it stretch the triangles between them, so a smile's
corners move the mouth. The warped pixels replace the held ones: nothing is requantised.

The warp costs nothing beyond the landmarks: both ends run `warp_faces` on the same picture
with the same landmarks, the encoder's from what it has sent and the decoder's from what it has
received. It is integer arithmetic throughout - the ring computed at twice size from the
landmarks so that a whole-pixel move of the face moves it exactly, an exact Bowyer-Watson
Delaunay triangulation with 64-bit predicates, barycentric mapping in fixed point, bilinear
sampling in 1/256 pixel steps - so two endpoints agree whatever compiler built them. Repeated
points, two landmarks on one pixel, are left out of the triangulation rather than producing
zero-area triangles.

Landmarks are not always right: a hand over the face or a lost track moves the mesh without
moving the face. The encoder therefore commits the warp only when it brings the blocks it
touches closer to the source than leaving them alone would, and the packet's header says
whether it did. Either way the landmarks are sent and become what the picture's face is taken
to be drawn at, so the next warp starts from them. When the number of faces or of their points
changes, the landmarks are sent afresh and nothing is warped.

On synthetic frames whose face moves by the same warp the codec predicts with - a mechanism
check, not a claim about real faces - the self test requires a drifting, smiling face to reach
the same picture for fewer bytes with landmarks than without. On the
[video-conferencing clips](#quality-evaluation) the warp is roughly neutral: talking heads
move little between ticks, and motion vectors follow them nearly as cheaply.

### Prediction

A delta packet predicts every macroblock it updates in one of three ways:

- **skipped**: moved along the vector its neighbours predict, and nothing more;
- **inter**: moved by a vector in quarter pixels - one for the macroblock, or one for each of
  its four 8x8 blocks - each block then corrected by a residual or not;
- **intra**: each 8x8 block predicted along one of H.264's nine 8x8 intra directions -
  vertical, horizontal, DC and six diagonals - from the decoded pixels above and to its left,
  smoothed by [1, 2, 1] as H.264 smooths them, and corrected by a residual. It is for a region
  nothing the receiver holds resembles, such as background a face has just uncovered: without
  it, a vacated region is corrected from the face that left it, and at a background's quantiser
  that leaves the face's texture behind.

A macroblock the packet does not update keeps what the receiver holds. Vectors point into the
picture as it stood before the packet, after any warp, so moved macroblocks do not depend on
one another; intra prediction reads the picture as the packet has rebuilt it so far, so a
block's neighbours above and to its left come first, as in H.264. The residual - the source
less the prediction - is transformed by an orthonormal 8x8 DCT, quantised, dequantised and
added back to the prediction, rounded and clamped, by the same code at both ends. A macroblock
that merely moved costs its vector and nothing else, and keeps its detail.

Half-pixel samples come from H.264's six-tap filter, (1, -5, 20, 20, -5, 1)/32, applied across,
down, and down over the unrounded across sums for the diagonal, over the whole held picture once
per packet. A two-tap average - the obvious choice - blurs: a block that follows a head is
resampled every packet it moves, and on a slowly moving newsreader the averages compounded into
a visibly softer face, with bits then spent restoring the detail its own predictor removed.
Quarter-pixel samples are the rounded mean of the two nearest whole- and half-pixel samples, as
H.264 makes them.

A vector is predicted as H.264 predicts it: the median of the vectors to the left, above and
above right - above left where above right is not decoded yet - when the packet moved all
three, the first of them it moved when it moved fewer, and otherwise the last vector the
packet sent. A skipped macroblock takes that prediction; any other sends its difference.

### Rate-distortion decisions

Each way of coding a macroblock is costed as

```
distortion + lambda * bits,   lambda = 0.15 * quantiser^2 per bit
```

with the macroblock's own quantiser, so a coarsely coded background is allowed to be sloppier
than a face. A macroblock whose error, left alone, is less than lambda times what addressing it
costs is not considered. For every other one the encoder tries skipping it, one vector -
searched, predicted and zero - with each block's residual sent only where it pays, a vector for
each block, and intra, and keeps the cheapest; a macroblock none of them can improve on leaving
alone is not a candidate.

Vectors are searched to minimise absolute error plus their cost in bits times the square root of
lambda, the multiplier for absolute rather than squared error, so a vector near its prediction
wins a near tie. The search starts from the best of the predicted vector, zero, the last vector
the packet chose and the one above, then steps a square pattern from eight pixels down to a
quarter; a block's own vector starts from the macroblock's and refines it within two pixels.
Of the nine intra directions, the three whose prediction errors have the smallest Hadamard
transforms, weighed with what naming them costs, and the probable direction are quantised and
costed in full; coding all nine measured no better. Four vectors and intra are not tried where
even their cheapest conceivable signalling, at no distortion at all, loses to what is already
found.

Coefficients are quantised with a dead zone - a level rounds up only past a third of a step in
intra residuals, 0.3 of a step in the others - and then every level of one or two, from the
highest frequency down, is lowered by one whenever the bits that saves are worth more than the
error it adds. Without the multiplier, a well predicted block would still cost a scatter of plus
and minus ones in requantisation noise every packet; with it, a scene that stops changing stops
costing bytes.

The candidate pass judges each macroblock as if every candidate before it were sent. Once the
packet's macroblocks are chosen, a final pass codes them in raster order: an inter macroblock
as the candidate pass chose it, sent as a skip wherever its vector turns out to be the
predicted one, and an intra macroblock's directions and residuals chosen afresh from the
picture as the packet has actually rebuilt it, which is what its receiver predicts it from.

The motion search, the interpolation, the transforms and the Hadamard ranking run on SSE2,
which every x64 processor has. A delta packet takes about 7 ms to encode on one core of a
recent desktop processor, and 15 ms at worst over the reference clips.

### The startup keyframe

The live wire emits one complete keyframe at startup, so the receiver begins with the whole
scene rather than neutral-grey blocks that fill in gradually. Every macroblock is intra coded
in raster order, each block's direction chosen by distortion plus lambda times bits but no level
ever lowered, so a keyframe is an exact intra coding of the picture, loop filter and all; the
self test compares it with `intra_picture`, which runs the same code without a wire, byte for
byte. It codes at the startup quality, is reported apart from the steady rate, and is not
subject to the cap. There are no periodic resets, so an established scene is never replaced by
grey.

### Entropy coding

Every syntax element after the header byte is coded by an adaptive binary range coder
(`entropy.h`) in the style of LZMA's: each binary decision against a model of how often it has
gone each way, kept as the mean of a fast and a slow estimate, so a decision that almost always
goes one way costs a small fraction of a bit. Integers are Exp-Golomb, their length's unary
prefix against models and the bits below it at even odds. The models are reset at a keyframe
and carried from packet to packet after it, which needs every packet to arrive - as the
held picture already does. The coder finishes with four bytes, and decoding must consume exactly
what was written, so truncation and padding are caught.

Residuals are coded much as H.264's CABAC codes them: a significance map in zigzag order, each
position with its own models for whether its coefficient is nonzero and whether it is the last,
apart for intra and inter residuals; then the levels from the highest frequency down - whether
each exceeds one, modelled on how many ones and larger levels came before - their remainders,
and signs at even odds.

Macroblock decisions are modelled on their neighbours, as CABAC models them: whether a
macroblock is updated on whether its left and upper neighbours are and on whether the previous
packet updated it, whether it is skipped or intra on how many of those neighbours are, and
whether a block carries a residual on how many of the blocks to its left and above do. An intra
block's direction is coded as whether it is the probable one - the lesser of its left and upper
neighbours' directions, DC for a neighbour that is not intra - and otherwise as one of the other
eight in three decisions.

### The loop filter

Once a packet's macroblocks are in, both ends run H.264's loop filter over every 8x8 block edge
touching a macroblock the packet updated - every edge, in a keyframe - macroblock by macroblock
in raster order, vertical edges before horizontal, so what it leaves is what the next packet
predicts from. An edge's strength is 4 where an intra macroblock meets another macroblock and 3
inside one, 2 where either side carries a residual, 1 where the two sides moved a pixel or more
apart - a block the packet left alone counting as still - and otherwise 0, nothing there being
the codec's doing. Its thresholds and limits are H.264's, indexed by the two blocks' mean
quantiser step plus 16, where the two quantiser ladders meet: a step across an edge smaller than
the quantiser explains is smoothed, across three pixels each way at strength 4 and by a clipped
amount nearer the edge otherwise, and a larger one is the scene's, and stays. The receiver shows
the filtered picture, enlarged with the same bicubic filter as H.264's.

### On the wire

A packet is one header byte - version 5 in the high nibble, then flags: bit 0 keyframe, bit 1
landmarks, bit 2 warp committed, a warp only with landmarks and neither in a keyframe - then,
for a keyframe, the coded width and height as two 16-bit values, then the range-coded payload:

| Section | Coded |
| ------- | ----- |
| landmarks | in a keyframe and whenever bit 1 is set: whether the layout changed; then either the faces' count, each face's point count and coordinates - the first point absolute, each other from the one before - or, for each face, whether it moved, its rigid shift and each point's residual from the shift |
| keyframe macroblocks | every macroblock in raster order: each block's intra direction, which blocks carry a residual, the macroblock's quantiser step as a change from the previous macroblock's, and the residuals |
| delta records | whether there are any; then for every macroblock in raster order whether it is updated, and for an updated one whether it is skipped, else whether it is intra. An intra macroblock is coded as in a keyframe, but sends its step only when a block carries a residual. An inter macroblock sends whether it has a vector for each block, its vectors as differences from their predictions, which blocks carry a residual, its step as a change from the last one sent if any does, and the residuals |

The rigid shift is the median of the face's points' motion, which is what the head did; the
residuals are what the expression did. A face that moved rigidly costs its shift, a smile its
mouth's corners: in the self test a whole packet carrying only a shifted face is 8 bytes, one
carrying a smile 7. A packet with neither landmarks nor records is never sent.

Decoding is transactional: the header's version and flags, the payload's structure and value
ranges, and its exact length are checked before decoder state is replaced. A self test checks
after every packet that the encoder holds exactly the picture, quantisers and landmarks the
decoder does.

### Landmarks

Before transmission the landmarks pass through a "one euro" filter (Casiez, Roussel and Vogel,
2012), whose cut-off rises with speed: 1.5 Hz for a still landmark, about 7.5 Hz for one moving
at 200 pixels a second. Raw landmarks jitter by a pixel from frame to frame, which costs bits
and makes a warped face shimmer, while a fast cut-off during real motion keeps the mouth from
lagging. The filter is sender-side only: the importance map and the landmark error use the raw
landmarks. Faces are paired by detector order - libmug's, left to right - not by a persistent
track identifier.

The Face priority button is the ablation: with it off there are no faces, so no face weighting,
no landmarks, no warp and no face offset for H.264.

## H.264 baseline

The third pane is H.264 from the encoder and decoder transforms that ship with Windows. It is a
real serialized wire: the picture shown is what the decoder made of the encoder's bytes, and
every byte the decoder needs - including the parameter sets, charged to the first frame if the
encoder keeps them out of band - is counted. Access unit delimiters and SEI messages are
dropped before counting, because the decoder needs neither: this encoder opens every frame with
a delimiter and its first frame with about 300 bytes of SEI, and charging them would handicap
the baseline.

It codes the same 320x240 luma as low-ban, with flat grey chroma that costs it next to
nothing, at a constant 20 kbit/s, the call rate low-ban is held to. Main profile with CABAC,
which is worth about a quarter of the bytes here; not High, because the in-box decoder loses
this encoder's low-latency High profile streams after the first few macroblocks of each slice.
Low-latency mode on both transforms, no B-frames, and a single key frame at the start like
low-ban's. At this rate the encoder's rate control misses its target on video-conferencing
clips: without the face offset it undershoots by 16 to 36%, and with it lands anywhere from
25% under to 13% over. `evaluate` reports what each wire actually spent.

It is fed the same face boxes the importance map is built from. The encoder takes one region
of interest per frame, so several faces share their union, and that region is coded six
quantiser steps finer than the rest - half the quantiser. `evaluate` runs H.264 with and
without the offset, so face priority can be judged on a competitive codec on its own, and
reports whether the encoder honoured the offset at all.

When the transforms are missing, the pane says why and the self test skips its H.264 checks
rather than failing. They can also be present and broken: the H.264 encoder registered on
GitHub's Windows Server 2025 runners crashes on its own worker thread during the first frame,
even with Server's Media Foundation feature installed. No error code reports the crash, and
no handler can recover from it, so before the first H.264 wire or clip opens, the program
runs one short round trip in a child copy of itself (`/probe-h264`), which suppresses the
crash dialog. If the child crashes or hangs, the encoder is unavailable for the rest of the
process: the pane, `evaluate` and clip recording say so, and the self test skips. Ordinary
failures, such as a missing transform, pass the probe and are reported by the call that meets
them.

H.264 frames are stamped at an even 15 frames a second. A camera that delivers fewer than 15
frames a second fires the clock less often, and the encoder, budgeting per frame, then spends
proportionally less than 20 kbit/s; the pane's measured rate shows it.

## Quality evaluation

Both received pictures are compared with the source luma, at the capture size, as the window
shows them. Each pane reports ordinary PSNR and importance-weighted PSNR, which uses the same
map as low-ban so an error around an eye or mouth counts more than the same error in the
background.

Neither says whether the face survived, so each pane also reports *landmark error*: the face
detector is run again on the received picture, each source face is matched to the received
face whose eyes are nearest its own, and the normalised mean error is the average distance
between the two sets of five points as a percentage of the source's eye spacing. A face the
detector cannot find within half its eye spacing counts as a whole eye spacing out, so a
receiver cannot score well by losing the face. Faces whose eyes are less than 24 pixels apart
are not scored: they are too small to carry an expression at the coded size, and the detector
finds them only some of the time - in the Johnny sequence a face on a poster behind the speaker
came and went, and decided the score until it was left out. The mouth's corners are also
scored alone in `evaluate`, since speech and expression move them most. Landmark error says
whether the face's geometry survived, not whether a viewer would read the expression; nothing
here measures that. Payload, quality and landmark error must be read together; a higher PSNR at
a much higher payload is not automatically better.

`dd.ps1 evaluate` has two parts.

- **Stills.** Every `samples/*.png` is resized to 640x480, detected and landmarked, and coded
  as low-ban's startup keyframe, landmarks and picture in one packet. H.264 then codes the same
  picture at the finest fixed quantiser whose single frame fits the same byte count, with and
  without the face offset. On the current nine samples, low-ban's startup averages 33.1 kbit
  at 34.88 dB, 34.67 dB face-weighted and 2.25% landmark error; H.264 at 32.2 kbit reaches
  35.09 dB, 34.66 dB and 3.67%, and with the face offset, at 31.0 kbit, 34.68 dB, 34.59 dB and
  2.96%. H.264 lands under the budget because whole quantiser steps are coarse; one finer step
  would overshoot it.
- **Clips.** Every `samples/*.mp4` and `samples/*.y4m` is replayed through two call sessions
  that differ only in whether H.264 gets the face offset. Each clip reports low-ban's startup
  size, steady rate, the landmarks' share of it, packets a second, link estimate, quality,
  landmark and mouth error and warped packets, and the same for both H.264 variants. Rates
  after startup exclude the startup pictures, which are reported on their own. This is the only
  measurement of motion, warp and scheduling.

The reference clips are Xiph's copies of standard video-conferencing test sequences, in
`video/videoconferencing` of the local test-media collection: the first 300 frames, five
seconds, of the 1280x720 `Johnny` (one speaker) and `KristenAndSara` (two), and the whole of the
CIF `akiyo` (a newsreader), `mother_daughter` (two faces) and `news` (two newsreaders and a
dancer on a screen behind them). At 20 kbit/s, with every rate as actually spent:

Low-ban's landmark error averages 7.0% over the five, H.264's 17.6% and 21.1% with the face
offset; low-ban's link estimate is about 35 kbit/s, payload, headers and audio. H.264 spends
less than low-ban without the offset, so the PSNR columns are not at equal rates.
Interpolating between H.264 runs at 12, 16, 20, 24 and 32 kbit/s, H.264 needs as many bits as
low-ban, on average, to reach low-ban's whole-picture PSNR at 20 kbit/s: 17% fewer on Johnny
and 14% fewer on akiyo, 3%, 17% and 19% more on KristenAndSara, mother_daughter and news.
Across the quality both reach between 12 and 32 kbit/s, low-ban needs 5% fewer bits than H.264
for the same PSNR, and 14% fewer for the same face-weighted PSNR. Its picture is level with a
mature codec's at the same bytes, and its faces are ahead because it spends the bytes there.
At 32 kbit/s the expression advantage narrows, and H.264 draws level on akiyo; at 16 kbit/s it
widens on four of the five and holds on Johnny.

What the coding tools are worth, added one at a time over the five clips at 20 kbit/s to
macroblocks with half-pixel vectors, DC intra prediction and no filter, which average 30.6 dB:
the loop filter 0.28 dB, the nine intra directions 0.13, quarter-pixel vectors 0.10, a vector
for each block 0.08, the rate-constrained vector search 0.08 and the skip 0.01, 0.7 dB in all.
The skip is worth little because low-ban leaves a macroblock that has not changed out of the
packet altogether, which is most of what H.264's skip is for.

## Face detection

Faces come from [libmug](https://github.com/ZacWalk/libmug), the face library Diffractor uses
too: YuNet - libfacedetection's network and weights - run on libmug's SIMD kernels, written
over Highway and dispatched at run time to the processor's best instruction set. libmug finds
faces on luminance alone, so `face_detector` hands it each frame's luma plane where it lies, at
video range, with no colour conversion, and asks for:

- the plane reduced to a long edge of 320 pixels, half a 640x480 frame: a webcam face is a
  hundred pixels or more across, so this costs nothing in recall and three quarters of the
  arithmetic;
- a confidence of at least 50 of 100, below libmug's default of 70, which suits photographs: a
  webcam face in poor light scores lower, and a face lost for a frame costs a geometry keyframe;
- at most eight faces, the most confident, in libmug's stable order: left to right, then top to
  bottom.

Each face comes back with the network's box, hairline to chin, and five landmarks: the eye
centres, the tip of the nose and the corners of the mouth. A 640x480 frame takes about 2.3 ms
on one core of a recent desktop processor, where the scalar port of the same network low-ban
used to carry took 26 ms, so detection runs on every frame, as does finding the faces again in
both received pictures.

libmug is built from a sibling `../libmug` checkout when there is one, or else from its pinned
commit, with `LIBMUG_RECOGNITION` off: low-ban never recognises anyone, so SFace's weights are
neither fetched nor linked.

## The expression prior

Five landmarks say where a face is, how it is turned and how wide it smiles. They cannot say
whether a mouth is open, a brow raised or an eye shut - and the receiver's warp, which moves
the face it holds by those five points, cannot reproduce any of that. Whatever a face does
beyond its five points can only reach the receiver as picture, so that is where the picture's
bits should go. The expression prior says where that is.

`tools/learn_expression_prior.py` measures it once, offline. A teacher that sees more than five
points - dlib's 68-point regression-tree model, run in numpy - landmarks Labeled Faces in the
Wild. Each face is aligned by five of its points, the same five libmug finds, on libmug's
template: the 112-pixel square `template_fit` carries every face's landmarks onto. A linear
model then predicts all 68 points from the five; what it cannot predict is what the five points
miss. Taken within each person's own pictures, so that the shape of someone's face does not
count as expression, that residual is mostly expression and pose. Each of the 51 points from
the brows down spreads its unexplained motion over the square as a Gaussian as wide as the
point's own spread across faces; the jaw line is left out, as it frames a face rather than
expressing anything and is the teacher's least certain stretch. The sum, scaled to 0 to 255
over a 28 by 28 grid, is the prior, compiled in as `src/expression-prior.cpp`.

Over 13,221 faces of 1,679 people with several pictures, the five points explain 76% of how a
person's 51 expressive points move between pictures. The picture change the rest makes is 58%
at the mouth, 37% at the brows, 3% at the eyes and 2% at the nose, so the prior is brightest
over the lips and then the brows. The eyes come out low because the teacher cannot see what
changes there: dlib's eye contour has no iris, so gaze is invisible to it, and LFW's posed
stills rarely catch a blink. A model-free check - how much the aligned picture itself varies
within a person - is no better a guide to expression: it is highest on the cheeks, where pose
and lighting move the shading, which is why the prior is measured through feature motion
rather than raw picture difference.

`add_face_importance` places it: libmug's `template_fit` maps each frame pixel near a face into
the square, the prior is sampled bilinearly there, and the pixel's importance rises from the
face's 96 towards 255 in proportion. Fitted to the face's own landmarks, the prior turns, scales
and moves with the head. The teacher was trained on iBUG 300-W, whose licence excludes
commercial use; it only measures, and nothing of it ships but the table.

To learn the prior again, install `tools/requirements.txt` (numpy and Pillow) into a virtual
environment and run the tool; it fetches LFW (180 MB) and dlib's model (64 MB) into
`tmp/expression-prior/` at their pinned SHA-256s, rewrites `src/expression-prior.cpp` and writes
a picture of the map beside the downloads.

## Bitrate figures

Both wires are held to 20 kbit/s of payload at 15 Hz. Low-ban's token bucket fills at 167 bytes
a tick and holds at most one 1,200-byte packet, so over any stretch it spends no more than the
call rate plus whatever a few landmark-only packets borrow; a still scene spends almost
nothing. H.264 runs at a constant 20 kbit/s and misses that target as
[described above](#h264-baseline). The headless checks hold low-ban's spending within the
bucket's deposits and every packet after startup inside its cap. All of this is serialized
payload; the link estimate adds headers and audio.

## Self test

`/test` runs without a camera or a window; `.\dd.ps1 test --label low-ban` runs it in both
configurations, and CTest adds a check that the executable embeds its application manifest.
It covers image operations including the bicubic enlargement, YUY2 and NV12 conversion
including bottom-up and padded buffers, the range coder, the low-ban wire, the loop filter, the
Delaunay triangulation and the warp, landmarks on the wire, the landmark filter and error, the
H.264 wire and its face offset, clip recording and replay, y4m replay, the call session's clock,
bucket and caps, face detection on a sample photograph, and the importance map the expression
prior makes. Checks that need Media Foundation's H.264 encoder - the H.264 wire, clip recording
and the session's H.264 receiver - are skipped, not failed, where the
[H.264 baseline](#h264-baseline) is unavailable, so CI does not exercise H.264.

`/test` also runs deterministic GUI-boundary checks without creating a visible window: the
client sizes, which the shell's display scaling must not shrink, the centred pane rectangles,
the frame tick and shared paint pass, the two buttons and the settings they flip and follow,
and the precedence and case handling of `/test`, `/evaluate` and `/probe-h264`. Command modes
never create the native frame, because the console path in `platform::run` returns first.

`/test` and `/evaluate` write to standard output when it is redirected, so CTest's log and the
`evaluate` result carry their text; run from a console, they attach to it instead.

Five checks are worth calling out, because they catch the failures that are otherwise silent:

- A face that keeps changing at a new position must not permanently outbid the macroblocks it
  vacated. The check moves a volatile textured square across a static background under a
  300-byte cap and requires the abandoned region to come back to within one level of the
  truth; ranking by importance first would leave a second copy of your head on a real camera,
  and without intra records the region would keep the face's texture. A companion check moves
  a detailed subject across a plain scene by two and a half pixels a packet and requires it to
  stay within 1.5 dB of its intra coding. A third holds a scene still and requires the wire to
  fall silent, which is what catches rate-distortion trimming mistaking its own rounding for
  new work.
- After every packet the encoder must hold exactly the picture, quantisers and landmarks the
  decoder does; a difference would compound silently, packet after packet.
- Landmarks that move over a still picture must be refused as a warp and cost no more than
  themselves, with the receiver keeping the picture it holds; and a rejected warped packet must
  leave the decoder exactly as a decoder that never saw it. Those are what keep a wrong
  landmark track from silently corrupting the picture at either end.
- libmug must find the face in a committed sample photograph, read as the camera delivers
  frames - luma at video range - with its eyes above its nose above its mouth, and must find it
  again unchanged; a blank picture must count as the face lost. That is what catches a range,
  stride or coordinate mistake between low-ban's planes and libmug's view, which synthetic
  frames, holding no face, cannot.
- The importance map must turn with a turned face. The prior is placed through the face's
  own landmarks, so a quarter turn of the five points must carry the mouth's importance with
  it exactly.

## Known limitations

What the current design cannot do:

- Only one camera is used, the first that activates, and only 640x480 YUY2 is requested.
- The encoder advances its picture, landmarks and entropy models assuming each packet is
  delivered. Every packet `encode` returns must reach `decode`, or the two sides start
  predicting from different pictures and decoding against different models, and neither
  resolves on its own; a real transport would need retransmission or a fresh keyframe.
- The warp is integer arithmetic, but the transform is a floating point DCT at both ends. In
  this process that is trivially identical; two endpoints built with different compilers could
  disagree by a rounding step.
- The warp mesh covers the face from chin to forehead. Hair, ears, neck, shoulders and hands
  are moved only by block motion compensation, and gestures get the background's weight.
- Five landmarks animate a head's motion and a smile's corners, nothing finer: a mouth that
  opens, a brow that rises or an eye that blinks reaches the receiver only in picture records,
  which is why the expression prior steers the picture's bits there.
- The expression prior was learned from stills of many people, not from video of one: it
  places the bits where faces vary beyond their five points across pictures, which is where
  expression lives, but it is a fixed map, not a measurement of the face on the call.
- The warp is committed or refused for a whole packet, not per face or per triangle.
- Vectors point into the one picture the receiver holds; there is one transform size, 8x8; the
  entropy models start from even odds at every keyframe rather than from trained values; and
  the encoder decides one macroblock at a time, greedily. Those are tools a mature codec has
  that low-ban lacks, and on Johnny and akiyo, the clips where face priority has least to win,
  its picture still trails H.264's at equal bytes.
- A macroblock's vector spans however many packets have passed since it was last updated, so
  neighbouring vectors agree less than in a codec that updates every macroblock every frame.
- Both codecs code luma only.
- H.264 takes a single region of interest per frame, so several faces share their union.
- Packets are serialized and decoded in memory but are not sent through a socket. The link
  estimate adds headers and audio by accounting, not by measurement.
- Multiple faces are paired by detector order, left to right. If two faces cross, the warp
  pairs the wrong faces for a packet, and is then refused.
- No recorded clip is committed, so motion results on a recording depend on whoever made it.
  The reference figures use public test sequences instead, which anyone can fetch.
